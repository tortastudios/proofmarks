# The Hardware Store Never Left

On a Tuesday morning in March, the line at Dobbins Hardware in Merriton runs past the key-cutting machine, past the bins of loose screws, and almost to the door. It is not a sale day. Nobody is giving anything away. People are here because the water heater went out, or the porch step finally rotted through, or the faucet in the upstairs bathroom has been dripping since Christmas and tonight is the night.

Walt Dobbins, who is seventy-one and has worked in this store since he was tall enough to see over the counter, watches the line with the calm of a man who has seen worse. "Everybody thinks the hardware store is dying," he says. "Nobody told the hardware store."

He has a point. For twenty years, the story of American retail has been a story of consolidation. Big-box chains moved to the edge of town, then online stores moved into everyone's pocket. Bookstores closed. Video stores vanished entirely. Department stores became apartment buildings or nothing at all. The small hardware store, with its narrow aisles and its creaking floors and its owner who knows your name, seemed like an obvious candidate for the next extinction.

It didn't happen. Not in Merriton, and not, it turns out, in a lot of places.

## A Business Built on Questions

The first thing you notice about Dobbins is that nobody comes in knowing exactly what they need. They come in with a broken part in a plastic bag, or a photo on their phone, or a description that begins with "it's sort of like a hinge, but."

"That's the whole business," says Rosa Delgado, who manages the store with Walt and will likely own it someday. "The big stores are great if you know the part number. We're for people who don't know the part number."

On the morning I visited, Rosa helped a man match a fifty-year-old radiator valve by laying it on the counter next to a dozen candidates until one fit. She talked a young couple out of replacing their entire garbage disposal, showing them the small hex key that would unjam it for free. She sold a retired teacher exactly four wood screws, counted out by hand, for thirty-two cents.

It's not just about selling products, it's about solving problems. And that difference, small as it seems, has turned out to be the moat that the chains could never cross.

## The Numbers Behind the Counter

The independent hardware store has proven remarkably resilient. Industry observers have noted that independent hardware retailers have held their ground even as other categories of small retail have collapsed. Many experts believe the reason is simple: home repair resists the logic of e-commerce. You can't download a plumber's advice. You can't wait two days for a part when water is coming through the ceiling.

Dobbins itself is a modest business. Walt won't share exact figures, but he says revenue has grown in each of the last six years, and the store's busiest year ever was during the pandemic, when everyone was home and everything was breaking. "People found out they could fix things," he says. "Some of them liked it."

The store's margins come from the things big chains can't do efficiently. Keys. Screen repair. Sharpening. Small quantities. Advice. A cooperative buying group, shared with about four thousand other independent stores across the country, gives Dobbins wholesale prices that are close enough to the chains' that customers rarely notice a difference on the shelf.

## Generations at the Register

What surprised me most was the age of the customers. I expected retirees, and there were plenty. But the line that Tuesday morning also included a woman in scrubs on her way to a hospital shift, two college students buying paint for a dorm mural, and a man in his early thirties who had just bought his first house and seemed slightly stunned by how many things in it could leak.

"We get a lot of first-time homeowners," Rosa says. "They grew up with landlords. Nobody ever showed them how to do any of this. So they come in scared, and we show them, and then they come back."

This is perhaps the most underappreciated part of the story. In a world increasingly mediated by screens, the hardware store offers something rare and valuable: a place where knowledge is passed from one person to another, face to face, in a way that builds confidence, competence, and community. It is a classroom without a curriculum, a library where the books talk back.

The young homeowner, whose name was Danny, had come in for a toilet flapper. He left with a toilet flapper, a pair of channel-lock pliers, and a five-minute lesson on how to shut off the water at the valve before he started. "I watched three videos last night," he told me on his way out. "This was faster."

## What the Chains Got Wrong

None of this means the chains are failing. They are enormous, profitable, and very good at what they do. If you are building a deck, you go to the big store on the highway and you buy a pallet of lumber at a price Dobbins could never match.

But the chains made an assumption that turned out to be wrong. They assumed that hardware was a commodity, that customers wanted the lowest price and the widest selection, and that service was a cost to be minimized. For a large share of purchases, they were right. For the rest, the small purchases attached to urgent problems, they were wrong, and that slice turned out to be big enough to sustain thousands of small stores.

Walt puts it more simply. "The big store sells you a drill," he says. "We tell you which bit."

## The Next Chapter

Like many independent businesses, Dobbins faces a succession question. Walt's children live in other states and have other careers. Rosa has worked at the store for fourteen years and has been, in practice, running it for the last five. The plan, Walt says, is for her to buy in gradually over the next several years.

Rosa is cautiously optimistic about the future. She has added a website where people can check whether an item is in stock, and she has started a Saturday morning workshop series, which she describes, a little sheepishly, as "Home Repair for People Who Are Afraid of Their Houses." The first session, on patching drywall, had nineteen people sign up. The second had thirty.

"I don't think we need to change much," she says. "We need to keep doing the thing we do, and make sure people know we're here."

Outside, the line has finally cleared. Walt is behind the counter, cutting a key for a woman who has locked herself out of her shed. The machine whines, and the brass shavings fall into the little tray beneath it, and for a moment the whole store smells like metal and sawdust and coffee.

The hardware store never left. We just stopped noticing it, until something broke.

## What We Can Learn

The lesson of Dobbins Hardware extends far beyond hardware. In an economy that often rewards scale above all else, there remains a vital place for businesses that offer expertise, attention, and human connection. These are the businesses that knit communities together, that turn customers into neighbors, and that remind us that some things can't be optimized away.

As consumers, we have a choice. Every time we walk into a store like Dobbins, we are voting for the kind of community we want to live in. That choice matters now more than ever.
